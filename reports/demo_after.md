# Synthetic revision demonstration: after

> **Synthetic fixture. These are invented demonstration data, not a report of a real study.**

The adaptive condition did not establish that an AI director increased immersion. Across the six
teams, the post-session median was 5.7/7 with adaptive direction and 5.4/7 with static pacing, but the
available packet provides neither an uncertainty estimate nor an inferential analysis at the
team/session level. The comparison is further bounded by two discomfort-related early exits that
were omitted from the paired rating summary. We therefore treat the ratings as a descriptive signal,
not evidence of effectiveness.

The interviews locate a more specific design contribution: adaptive direction redistributed control
over *when* a scene should move forward. Fourteen participants could identify an intervention that
restored momentum, while seven also described an intervention as making the scene feel less like
their own. That tension cannot be reduced to a generic claim that AI “empowered” players. Moreover,
the director did not act autonomously: it generated 72 proposals, a researcher adjudicated every
proposal, and only 44 were enacted. The observed experience was thus produced by players, model
proposals, and human curation together.

These findings motivate **author-constrained improvisation** as the object of design: an adaptive
director should help a group recover stalled narrative motion without silently taking authorship
away from it. A next study should preregister the team/session-level immersion contrast, retain all
assigned sessions in the primary analysis with a stated missing-data rule, report discomfort as an
outcome, and manipulate the visibility or reversibility of proposed interventions. That design would
test whether the benefit attributed to adaptive pacing depends on participants being able to
recognize, contest, and undo the director's move.
